Most people who can build AI features can't verify them. Most people who can verify software can't build AI. That gap is the whole job.

Think about who's actually shipping LLM features right now. Strong builders. They can wire up an agent, a RAG pipeline, a generator, and get a demo working fast. Ask them "how do you know it's right across a thousand inputs?" and the answer is a shrug and a few screenshots. Not because they're careless — because verification is a different discipline than construction, and nobody trained them in it.

Now look at classical QA. Deep testing instinct, but a lot of it stalls at AI. Non-deterministic outputs, no fixed expected value, model-graded checks — it breaks the muscle memory of "assert equals." So they stay away.

I've spent years on both sides. Nine at Home Depot, five cutting flake and hardening suites at a trading-education company, and the whole time getting certified specifically in AI testing. My entire position is that intersection: someone who can read the code, run the code, and prove the AI feature does what the demo promised.

I don't think this gap closes soon. Building AI is getting easier every month. Proving it works is not. So the demand isn't for more builders — it's for people who can stand behind the output.

Prove it. No vibes. That's the standard, and it's the opportunity.

## Why this works
A manifesto that names the exact market gap Jason occupies and frames his unusual background (QA + AI + building) as the rare qualification rather than a resume list. No CTA — pure positioning, which makes it feel like conviction, not marketing.
